Case study 02 · Product strategy

A first year as Group Product Manager, written down

Two last-mile logistics products, ten countries, one sub-vertical and no shared strategy between them. This is the plan I wrote for the first year: an honest read of where both products actually stood, a 2027 vision with guardrails, four pillars, and a 90/180/360-day plan where every phase commits to outcomes and names the risk that could stop it.

Scope
Partner & courier interaction — 2 products
Footprint
10 countries, marketplace & own-logistics
Method
SWOT, vision canvas, RICE-style scoring, metrics tree
Output
12-month roadmap, 90/180/360 plan, team operating model

Written for a Group Product Manager process at a pan-European food delivery group. Absolute revenue figures, prospective partner names and internal system names have been removed; the structure, the reasoning and the relative impact figures are as submitted.

The remit

Partner/courier interaction is the layer where a delivery actually happens: the partner hands over the order, the courier picks it up, the customer watches it move. It sat across two very different products.

  • A grocery pick-and-pay product. A shopper-courier walks a store aisle, scans items, handles substitutions and pays with a company card. A strong MVP in one market, built on a platform that needs replacing before it can go anywhere else.
  • A partner-courier tracking platform. A courier app plus a partner portal that lets restaurants with their own couriers plan routes, assign orders and expose live tracking to the customer. Already at real scale: 10 countries, ~85,000 monthly active couriers, ~25,000 monthly active partners, 4M+ orders a month.

One product needed a rebuild and a market-entry decision. The other needed adoption it could not mandate — partners use it voluntarily. Those are opposite problems, and the first job was to say so out loud rather than write one roadmap that pretended otherwise.

Reading the ground before proposing anything

I ran a SWOT on each product, not as a formality but to force a shared, 360-degree view of reality with people who each held one quarter of it. The uncomfortable entries are the useful ones.

The grocery pick-and-pay product

Strengths

  • Strategically critical for attracting and retaining key partners in its home market.
  • MVP with real traction — hundreds of thousands of orders and a proven basket.
  • Rich feature set: dedicated picking app, barcode scanning, payment integration, item substitution.
  • Redesigned picking app tested well with couriers.

Weaknesses

  • Built on a legacy platform; a full rebuild is a precondition for expansion.
  • Roadmap is clear but too broad — we cannot do vertical and horizontal expansion at once.
  • 14% of orders arrive incomplete because of out-of-stock items.
  • Missing features have been outright deal-breakers with prospective partners.
  • Pick-and-pay takes longer than a restaurant delivery, so cost per order blocks rollout in high-labour-cost markets.

Opportunities

  • Weighted items (sold by kilo) unlocks the large grocery chains outright.
  • New categories beyond grocery: drugstores, liquor, convenience.
  • Single-partner pooling to cut cost per order and make expensive markets viable.
  • Closing the feature gap gives a credible pitch against incumbents.

Threats

  • Established pick-and-pack competitors have set partner expectations high.
  • Major retailers have hard requirements we do not yet meet.
  • Platform migration carries unforeseeable technical risk.
  • A large, cross-country stakeholder group means conflicting priorities and slow decisions.

The partner-courier tracking platform

Strengths

  • Significant penetration: 10 countries, 4M+ orders a month.
  • Proven impact — 42% lower cancellation rate, 19% fewer order-status contacts, 22% more tipped orders for partners using it.
  • Lifts restaurant delivery rating by 2.73% on average.
  • A complete toolkit on both sides: a partner portal and a courier app.

Weaknesses

  • Usage is voluntary, which caps how hard adoption can be pushed.
  • Sales, account management and marketing are not actively driving partner awareness.
  • No joint go-to-market strategy with the commercial org.
  • Expansion depends on other teams' roadmaps — a real delivery risk.

Opportunities

  • Chain partners: optimising for multi-location access would move tracked orders massively.
  • Deeper integration with the in-restaurant order pad and kitchen staff access.
  • Becoming the single logistics dashboard — third-party fleets and delivery-as-a-service included.
  • More markets, on an already-proven product.

Threats

  • Competitors ship comparable or more advanced logistics tooling.
  • Adoption is the whole game; any friction stalls growth.
  • At this volume, downtime hits thousands of partners and millions of customers immediately.

The vision, and the guardrails on it

By 2027, partner/courier interaction will be the universal last-mile orchestration layer that makes every order reliable, economical and compliant — anywhere we operate.

A vision without a number is a slogan, so it comes with business outcomes and with guardrails that constrain how they may be reached.

80%of all marketplace orders tracked by the platform
3×grocery order volume, as the sole app for grocery delivery wherever we operate
−20%cost per order — a guardrail, not a goal to trade away
70%tracked-order floor held throughout

Four pillars

Every initiative on the roadmap belongs to exactly one of these, and each pillar owns its own metrics. If an idea doesn't fit a pillar, it doesn't get a quarter.

Scalability & growth

New markets for both products, and multi-location access so chain partners can adopt the tracking platform at all.
Metrics: order volume, active partners, active couriers.

Monetisation & ecosystem

On-demand courier, a premium tier, and turning the partner portal into the one dashboard for every logistics model a partner uses.
Metrics: partner acquisition, active partners, gross transaction value.

Courier & partner experience

One courier flow across delivery models, partner–courier chat, AI-assisted picking and smarter order assignment.
Metrics: CSAT, partner retention, active couriers.

Cost efficiency

Single-partner pooling, card-authorisation and geofencing work against courier fraud, and cutting the reasons customers contact support at all.
Metrics: cost per order, contact propensity, missed orders.

How I decide what goes first

Prioritisation is where a group roadmap either holds together or turns into four teams lobbying. I run it as an explicit equation so the argument is about the inputs rather than the outcome:

[ Impact on the org's deciding metric × urgency against competition × stakeholder alignment ] ÷ effort

Every candidate has to name a deciding metric and an estimated effect on it before it gets scored at all. The real value of the equation is that it makes the long-term/short-term trade-offs visible instead of implicit:

Two live trade-offs, and where I landed.
ObjectiveLong-term choiceShort-term choiceDecision
Growing the grocery product Build the features that unlock new partner acquisition and dominate the home market. Slower growth, higher effort. Integrate with the shared platform sooner. Faster growth, more tech debt. Short term. The platform migration is a precondition for everything else on the roadmap.
Expanding the tracking platform Launch in both target markets at once. Higher annual tracked orders, sooner market capture. Ship the sign-in migration first for a better courier experience and less friction. Long term. Tracked orders is the pillar metric, and a staged launch defers the compounding.

The first year, in three phases

90 days

Foundation & quick wins

Objectives

  • Get fluent in the ecosystem, the stakeholders and the data.
  • Health-and-risk review of every critical in-flight initiative.
  • Define the long-term growth strategy for both products.
  • Run a feature-gap analysis against the competition.
  • Find the process bottlenecks and propose fixes.
  • Build trust — give PMs ownership and autonomy.

Committed outcomes

  • A decision on direction: vertical expansion (new strategic partners, chains) versus horizontal (new markets), assessed on product-market fit and cost feasibility.
  • A named feature set that closes the competitive gap.
  • Delivered: weighted items and the SKU increase; courier activity data and the first wave of market launches.

Risks & mitigations

  • Weighted items depends on another team → align early and scope a trimmed version of their contribution.
  • International rollout depends on the shared-app team → revisit timelines and get an explicit commitment.
  • Sign-in migration is the gate on two market launches.
180 days

Platformise & expand

Objectives

  • Launch single-partner pooling.
  • Launch both products in the new markets.
  • Make the tracking platform chain-ready.
  • Ship the features that close partner deals.
  • Evolve and empower the PM team.

Committed outcomes

  • Two national grocery chains live on the product.
  • Cost per order down 15% after pooling ships.
  • Tracked orders up 20% against baseline; chain users can access multiple locations.
  • A written growth plan for every PM in the team.

Risks & mitigations

  • Messaging-vendor load at new volume → size the increase and renegotiate quota ahead of launch.
  • Adoption stalls in new markets without commercial support → agree a joint strategy with country management first.
  • Courier card fraud → geofenced authorisation payments.
360 days

Value & excellence

Objectives

  • Flexible logistics: let partners switch delivery model per order rather than close.
  • Deep retail readiness — SKU database to 100K.
  • Globalisation: expand grocery into Europe; mature adoption in every new market.

Committed outcomes

  • On-demand courier live.
  • Twice as many orders tracked as at the start.
  • 15% of chain orders tracked through the platform.
  • Grocery order volume doubled.

Risks & mitigations

  • Platform migration risk → feature flags and a rollback path per market.
  • Cost per order too high in new grocery markets → efficiency measures with CPO monitored as a release gate.

How I'd run the group

A group PM role is two-thirds operating model. Mine has three parts, and none of them are optional.

  • Communication and alignment. Releases communicated outward across the org, and a real feedback loop from partners back into the product teams — not a survey nobody reads.
  • Data and insights. Consistent KPI definitions across both products, and maintained dashboards per team. Two products reporting "orders" differently is a strategy problem disguised as a reporting problem.
  • Tools and process. Standardised initiative reporting aligned with the wider logistics org, PRDs and documentation that actually exist, and deliberate removal of friction in the product development lifecycle.

On the team itself: ownership and accountability with clear goals and then genuine stepping back; career paths co-created rather than assigned; a feedback culture where giving and receiving is frequent enough to be unremarkable; and recognition that is specific and personal rather than a quarterly ritual.

What this case is really about

The easy version of this document is a roadmap. The version worth writing states what is being traded away — the growth we defer by migrating a platform first, the tech debt we take on by not, the markets that stay closed until another team commits. Every phase here names the dependency that could stop it and who I'd have to go and align with. That is the part of the job that doesn't fit on a Gantt chart, and it's the part I'd want to be judged on.
