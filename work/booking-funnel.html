Case study 01 · Booking funnel

Three quarters of visitors never ask if the trip is available

A conversion teardown for a travel-experiences marketplace. One click separates a 0.20% booking rate from a 17.47% one — and nothing about it is a payment problem. This is how I found it, why I bet the roadmap on the biggest category rather than the worst one, and what would have told me I was wrong.

Role
Sole author — analysis, UX audit, recommendations
Input
2,014-session dataset, 13 categories, 14 days
Method
Funnel analysis, competitive walkthrough, RICE, A/B design
Output
3 recommendations, 1 test design, measurement plan

Written as a take-home exercise on a sample dataset supplied by the company. The platform is left unnamed here; the analysis, the numbers and the reasoning are exactly as I submitted them. Competitor observations come from walking the public products myself.

The answer, in five lines

Of 2,014 product-page sessions, 75% never click "Check availability." That one click separates a 0.20% booking rate from a 17.47% one. Once a cart exists, 70% of them become bookings.

  1. The funnel has two big leaks, and neither of them is checkout.
  2. I chose Day Trips on money at stake, not on the worst rate: 23.4% of sessions, 35.1% of booked value, €110 average booked price.
  3. The Day Trips gap sits entirely at the availability gate — 20.55% against 26.01% for the other twelve categories. Below the gate it beats the platform on every step. Demand is fine; the page is where this breaks.
  4. A day trip is a route, and the platform treats it as a place. Destination, travel time, pickup point and what's included all sit behind the "Check availability" button.
  5. Fix search first, then fix the page. Sessions that reached the options already date-resolved added to cart at 37.8% against 16.7%.

Where the funnel actually leaks

Four steps, and the four rates multiply out to the end-to-end number exactly. The largest single loss is 1,516 sessions that never ask whether the trip is available — four times the size of the second leak. Payment is the healthiest part of the funnel.

A detour worth taking: the calendar isn't a funnel step

The brief implied a sequence — people picker, then calendar, then date selection, then availability. Run as a sequence it breaks: the funnel would have to expand twice, which is impossible. They are interactions inside the availability module, and only 10 of the 90 bookings complete that chain.

Read as behaviour rather than as steps, they say something the funnel view hides.

Why Day Trips, and not the categories that convert worse

The temptation is to fix the worst rate. Transfers converts at 1.28% and Multi-day at 1.33% — on one booking each, so those rates are noise, and between them they produced €105 of booked value. Fixing them moves nothing. Walking Tours has the worst gate rate of all (19.29%) and the lowest value per session of any sizeable category (€0.51).

What the traveller actually runs into

I booked a day trip out of Rome on three platforms and stopped before paying. A generic heuristic sweep finds problems on any page; I used three lenses that find the problems specific to a fixed-departure, option-priced, high-commitment product — which is what a day trip is.

  1. Information clarity — is the answer to the traveller's next question available before they have to commit to anything?
  2. Cognitive load — how much work is it to compare the options and pick one?
  3. Expectation matching — does what I was promised match what I'm shown when it counts?

Search

  • Cards carry no logistics. Title, duration, rating, a from-price. Not departure time, meeting point, whether pickup is included, group size, or the next available date — the four things that define a day trip. You can't shortlist; you have to open product pages one at a time.
  • Filters are activity-generic, not category-native. On "day trips from Rome" the chips were Golf carts, For adults, For kids, Food & drink, Tours on wheels, Photoshoots. None of them are destination, departure window, pickup or group size. A one-hour basilica entrance ticket ranked on page one.
  • The initial search field asks for a destination and nothing else, which drops the traveller into a sea of near-identical options and makes comparison the traveller's job.

Product page

  • Repeated input. Arriving with a date already set from search, you still have to click "Check availability" — and group size doesn't carry to the next product page. Comparing three trips means entering the same two things three times. Effort signals difficulty, and invisible friction becomes a real conversion killer.
  • The page answers "is pickup included?" three different ways — the header says it depends on the option, the includes block lists hotel pickup right beside the exclusions, and the meeting point names a fixed square. It's the highest-anxiety question in the category and there is no single answer.

Checkout

  • The price you're promised is not the price you're shown. A €81 advertised "from" price; €96.30 for the cheapest option actually bookable — +19% — with the sidebar still saying €81 on the same screen.
  • Option paralysis. Three options at €96.30 / €152.00 / €1,900.00 — a 20× spread — with near-identical names differing by one town, truncated descriptions, no recommendation and no per-option availability. 51.5% of Day Trips availability-checkers check more than once; only 24.7% of them add to cart.
  • An identity wall before logistics certainty. The first checkout screen asks for an email address under a 30-minute countdown, with no guest path, and the order summary never confirms the meeting point. We ask a traveller to identify themselves and pay for a 7am, twelve-hour, €96 commitment while "where do I stand at 7am" is still open.

Three recommendations

1

Date-first search, carried through to the product page

Date and group-size fields in initial search, pre-filled rather than empty, carried with the click. The product page opens already priced and bookable, and "Check availability" becomes "Change dates". Products with no availability in the window drop out of the result set rather than appearing and failing later. Party size persists page to page.

Hypothesis
If purchasability is resolved before the traveller picks a product, more sessions reach a cart — because attention is spent only on trips that can actually be bought.
Primary metric
Visit → add to cart. Day Trips baseline 5.08%, platform 5.96%.
Estimated impact
+21% relative. Ceiling is the observed 37.8% (date-resolved arrivals) against 16.7% — 13.1pp of headroom. Capturing 25 / 40 / 60% gives +13% / +21% / +32%.
What would make me wrong
Dropping unavailable products removes sessions that convert at 0% today, so the rate can rise with no extra carts. Watch absolute carts per search, not the rate.
2

The "Day Plan" card, above the fold

A fixed card answering the four questions that decide a day out — departure window, return window, travel time versus time on site, and a two-column included / not-included list — all resolved before a date is chosen. Pickup resolved to one of three honest states: hotel pickup in named zones, meeting point only, or pickup time confirmed after booking. Anything but "depends on the option."

Evidence
375 of 472 Day Trips sessions (79.4%) never click Check availability, and zero of them book. Post-gate, Day Trips already beats the platform on every measure.
Primary metric
Visit → availability-check rate. Baseline 20.55% against a 24.73% platform rate.
Estimated impact
+5 to +8% relative on category conversion, capturing 25–40% of the 4.2pp gate headroom. Same card fits Bus Tours and Multi-day Trips — together 39% of the sample.
What would make me wrong
We are making a promise. If suppliers don't meet it, we have bought conversion with refunds — which is why cancellation rate blocks the ship rather than getting monitored.
3

The no-match rescue

A day trip runs on fixed days from a fixed point, so "no suitable option" is a routine outcome, not an edge case — and today it ends the session. On no-match only, show the nearest bookable departures for the same product, same-route products from other suppliers on the traveller's date, and the operating pattern in plain words: "runs Tue, Thu, Sat."

Hypothesis
The traveller has lost the route to the trip, not the interest in it.
Primary metric
Availability check → add to cart. Day Trips baseline 24.74%.
Estimated impact
+17 to +21% relative, if no-matches are ~15% of availability sessions and the module recovers a quarter to a third of them.
What would make me wrong
It argues against my own cognitive-load lens if shown by default — it must be triggered by failure only. And it sends people off the page by design, so page conversion is the wrong read.

Confidence is where the honesty lives. Rec 1 sits at 70% because its strongest data point — date-resolved sessions add to cart at 37.8% against 16.7% — carries a real confound: selecting a date may be a marker of intent rather than a cause of conversion. That is precisely why I took 25% of the headroom rather than 40%, and why I put no euro figure on it. Rec 3 sits at 40% because Day Trips converts availability → cart above the platform average, which is itself the cleanest evidence that Recs 1 and 2 target the right step.

The experiment

One change, two variants, an even split, and a primary metric chosen before launch.

  • Control: today's search. The date filter lives in second-layer filters and availability still needs a manual click.
  • Treatment: initial search asks Where, When and Who; results are trimmed to those parameters; clicking through carries them and resolves availability automatically.
  • Audience: every session landing on search — all devices, new and returning.
  • Primary KPI: visit → cart, platform baseline 5.96%. MDE +13% relative. Sample 15,545 per variant, minimum two full weeks so both weekend cycles are covered.

Guardrails — any breach blocks the ship

  • Absolute carts per 1,000 sessions, not just the rate. Removing unavailable products removes sessions that convert at 0% today, so the rate can rise on a smaller, better-matched population.
  • Products viewed per session and supplier feedback. A filter that hides supply is a commercial decision, not only a design one.
  • SEO and paid: indexed listing coverage, organic landing sessions, landing-page bounce.
  • Cart → booking, to catch carts built on dates the traveller didn't really choose.

Decision rule, written before launch

Assumption check first. The share of product-page arrivals that are date-resolved must rise sharply from its 38% baseline. If it doesn't, the feature didn't ship and the test is void. Then: if that share rises and visit → cart does not move, date selection was a marker of intent and not a cause — I read a correlation as a mechanism, and I would say so rather than re-cut the data until it agreed with me.

What I'd watch after it ships

The trip happens weeks after the booking, so most of the risk in this change lands after the experiment window closes.

Post-launch measurement plan.
WhenWhat I measureWhy it matters
Week 0–8 Hold 10% of traffic on control. Tells me whether the effect lasts, and leaves a clean baseline if a guardrail moves later.
Week 1 Re-baseline the availability gate — the event now fires without traveller action. Shipping this quietly destroys the meaning of the metric Rec 2 was measured on, and six months later someone tests that step and reads noise.
Week 2–8 Rolling 60-day cohort keyed to travel date: cart → booking, cancellation and refund rate, stale-availability errors. A date carried automatically might not be a date anyone actually chose. That shows up as refunds, weeks after the test closed.
Ongoing Supplier impressions and organic landing sessions. Hiding unavailable products permanently changes what is discoverable.

What this case is really about

The dataset offered an easy answer — fix the worst-converting category — and a comfortable one: blame checkout. Neither survives contact with the numbers. What the analysis actually rewards is asking which loss is large, which one you can explain, and which explanation you could be wrong about. The final deck says out loud where a correlation might be masquerading as a mechanism, and names the reading of the results that would make me retract the recommendation. That's the part I'd want a hiring manager to read.
